My story

It started with a screwdriver.

My mom tells me I used to take my toys apart with a screwdriver just to see how they worked. Putting them back together? That was another story.

That curiosity eventually led me to computers. Video games got me interested, and before long I was learning HTML, CSS and JavaScript and building websites. I even set up my own Ragnarok Online server. It never had many players, but that wasn’t really the point. I had fun learning Linux, setting up port forwarding and figuring out how to get everything running.

I studied Computer Science, but my first job in tech was in support, digging through logs, debugging issues and figuring out why things broke. I knew I wanted to be a developer, and it took a couple of years of pushing for the opportunity before I finally made the move.

I didn't stop learning once I got there. I worked on side projects, studied software design principles and practised coding interviews in my own time. Eventually, I moved to another company as an intermediate developer and kept taking on bigger challenges. I worked my way into a senior developer role and was later promoted to Technical Architect. Today, I'm trusted with some of the team's toughest technical problems and decisions. I'm proud of how far I've come, but there's always more I want to learn.

Engineering & leadership

How I work.

I spend a lot of time thinking about the decisions behind the code: what will work now, what might cause trouble later, and how to make things easier for the people working on it.

01

Seeing the whole picture.

When I’m designing something, I don’t stop at the application code. I think about how it connects to APIs, data, infrastructure and deployment—and whether the team will be able to maintain it without headaches later.

⌘
02

Helping developers grow.

I enjoy being someone developers can bounce ideas off. When someone is stuck, I’d rather help them work through the problem than just hand over an answer. I want people to feel confident making decisions on their own.

↗
03

Getting to the root of it.

Some of my favourite work starts with a bug nobody can quite explain. I follow it wherever it leads—code, databases, integrations or servers—until I understand what’s really happening.

□
04

Keeping things practical.

I don’t think every problem needs a big redesign. Sometimes the better choice is a smaller change that’s easier to understand, deliver and maintain.

◇

A few results

Some work I’m proud of.

Most of my work happens behind the scenes. Here are three examples that stand out to me, without getting into private client details.

20 min → milliseconds

From minutes to milliseconds

One page I worked on was taking roughly 20 minutes to load. The problem came down to oversized data transfer objects. After changing how that data was structured, the page-load time dropped to milliseconds.

30% → 80%+

Better test coverage, together

My team increased JUnit test coverage from around 30% to more than 80%. I’m proud of what we achieved together.

500+ stores

Automating flyer data

I worked on automating the flow of digital flyer data into a retail platform serving more than 500 stores. It’s the kind of behind-the-scenes integration work that makes a large system easier to run.

Electronics experiment

Motion-aware smart mirror

I built a smart mirror to show things like the time, calendar and weather. Getting the screen to respond to motion was trickier than I expected. HDMI-CEC wasn’t working with the display, so I tried a motion sensor and infrared control instead. Getting the IR signal right turned into its own little project.
