Expertise

Where I can help.

I can help when you’re trying to change a system without breaking what already works, or when something keeps going wrong and nobody’s quite sure why.

Software & solution architecture

I like getting a messy set of requirements down to a few decisions a team can actually build from. What belongs together? What needs to change independently?

Backend systems & integration

When I’m connecting two systems, I want to know who owns the data and what happens if one side stops answering. That second question tends to come up eventually.

Production troubleshooting & modernization

If a problem keeps coming back, I want to find out what’s causing it. When the fix means changing an older system, I look for a first step the team can deliver and learn from.

Engineering guidance

I enjoy talking through a design with the people who’ll build it. A good review gives us a chance to ask questions while the answer is still easy to change.

From developer to architect

I still think like someone in support

When we’re talking through a design, I think about the person who’ll get the call when it breaks. What will they see in the logs? What will help them figure out what happened?

I started looking beyond the application

As a developer, I could spend most of my time getting one application to work well. Moving into architecture meant thinking more about what that change would mean for the other systems and teams around it.

I like seeing people find their own answers

One of my favourite parts of mentoring is when someone comes back with an option I hadn’t considered. It gives us something real to talk through together.

My story

It started with a screwdriver.

Apparently, I had a habit of taking my toys apart as a kid just to figure out how they worked. Putting them back together? That was another story.

I grew up playing Neopets and thinking Windows XP was the best thing ever. Games got me into computers. I built my own PC, taught myself HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, and hosted a Ragnarok Online server. It didn’t have many players, but half the fun was learning Linux, sorting out the networking, and getting the thing to stay online.

After studying Computer Science, I started in technical support. I liked digging through logs and tracing a problem back to its source. I also wanted to be the person who could change the software that caused it. I kept learning alongside the day job until I got my chance to move into development.

That eventually led me to technical architecture. I’m proud of the work it took to get there. And I’m a lot better at putting things back together now.

My approach

How I work.

Follow the evidence.

I start with one example I can follow. When did it fail? What was different that time? Once I can explain what happened, it’s much easier to know what to check after a fix.

Write down the trade-offs.

I like writing down why we picked an option while the conversation is still fresh. Sometimes it comes down to what the team can support today or what we can learn from a smaller change.

Leave the reasoning with the team.

If someone has to extend a design six months later, I want them to know what we were thinking. A short note from a review can save them a lot of guessing.
